Genus

A first-person puzzle game where each level is a strange flat surface: rooms that wrap around, seams that mirror you, and portals that grow or shrink you. A quail is the one thing that never changes size.

Prototype  Last updated October 27, 2026 · Try the prototype

The idea

A Doom-style first-person game: every level is a flat floor plan with heights (stairs, ledges, sills), and gravity always holds you to the floor. What's unusual is how the level is glued together. Its edges are portals that join up into a torus, a Möbius band, or a surface where some loops make you bigger or smaller. The player never sees a map of the topology; they learn it by walking, looking, and carrying things around.

It's a puzzle game, not a shooter. The main verb is picking up, carrying and putting down items.

Three kinds of portal

PortalWhat it doesThrough the doorway
WraparoundTranslation only. The world loops (cylinder, then torus). You can see around the loop.The same room repeating.
FlipMirrors the world without resizing (Möbius / Klein seams).A mirrored room.
ScalingA 2:1 or 1:2 portal: you and what you carry shrink or grow relative to the world.A bigger or smaller room.

All portals can be open doorways: you see the room beyond already transformed, so stepping through causes no visual jump. A doorway 2 units wide on your side and 1 on the far side shows the far room twice as big, because you'll be half size there. Curtains, falling water or tapering passages are optional style choices, not requirements.

Per door, the designer can make the change obvious (the far room uses the usual furniture, so it looks giant or tiny through the door) or hidden (the far room is built at the matching scale, like a dollhouse, so it looks normal; the change shows only in carried items, familiar objects, or the quail, which never scales).

Later portals combine flip and scaling. Each loop through the level has a net scale and a net flip, depending only on the route taken. In log₂ units a 2:1 portal is +1 or −1, so sizes are whole numbers and levels can be checked by a solver.

Size

  • No HUD. You learn your size only from your surroundings: familiar crates, chairs, doors, and the quail.
  • A grid the size of your body. You walk one square at a time, like a chessboard, but a square is one body-width: 1 unit at normal size, ½ at half size, and so on. Movement chains smoothly while you hold a direction and always ends at the centre of a square. Walls and doorways sit on grid lines at every allowed size, and glued doorways map grid lines to grid lines.
  • Four sizes: double, normal, half and quarter (quail-riding size) for most levels. An eighth is reserved for the finale.
  • Crates are never carried. A crate one square wide (your size) can be pushed or pulled one square at a time (walk into it to push; hold to grab and step back to pull). A crate half your size is a step you can climb onto. A crate bigger than you is a wall. The same crate changes role as you change size, and it keeps its size in the world.
  • Climbing: you can step up half your own height. A sill or ledge can therefore be a size gate.
  • Collecting is automatic: walk onto a specimen to log it.
  • Doors have a size window. Too small: can't reach the handle or step over the threshold. Too big: can't fit through. Near the top of the window you crawl.
  • One-way sills. A threshold with a step on one side and a ramp on the other. Placed so routes toward shrinking close behind a size floor, which means walking through the wrong door can never leave you stuck too small.
  • Speed scales with size. Steps take the same time at every size, so small you covers less ground.

Everyday doorways and openings

Size gates should look like ordinary architecture, not puzzle machinery.

  • Threshold lips: a raised lip too tall for small you to climb.
  • Heavy push doors: only open once you're big enough to push them.
  • Saloon-style swinging doors: swing shut behind you, so you can end up on the wrong side; a crate can prop one open.
  • Deep adobe window sills: a window you can crawl through only at regular size. Small, you can't reach it; big, you don't fit. Sills also work as ledges to climb onto.

Handedness

  • From your own view you never flip; after a flip portal, the world looks mirrored. Something that didn't come with you looks mirrored when you return to it.
  • No text in the game. Signs are simple symbols. Chiral ones (arrows, moons, spirals, footprints) get mirrored and tell you about your path; symmetric ones (circles, stars, plus signs, up arrows) mean the same however you got there.
  • Tetromino keys and locks (L/J, S/Z): a key fits only after taking a route with the right flip parity.
  • Door hinge side as a quiet, built-in chirality cue.
  • Moon phases for some puzzles or as decoration (waxing vs. waning is a mirror pair).

The quail

  • A scaled quail (Callipepla squamata), nicknamed “cottontop” or “blue quail”: scaly blue-grey feathers and a white tufted crest. A runner rather than a flier, living in coveys. Right for a game about scaling.
  • One quail buddy, met full-grown partway through the game (no hatching).
  • It goes through portals with you and flips with you, but never changes size. It's your ruler. The reason is a leg band it wears from the start, with a tiny clasp too small to handle at any normal size. The band is on one leg, so it also shows mirroring.
  • At quarter size or smaller you can ride it, and it's fast. At other sizes it follows you. It can't be picked up.
  • It never blocks you: if you (or a crate you push) are about to land where it stands, it hops out of the way.
  • It moves on the quarter-size grid and is stopped by exactly what stops you at quarter size. Riding, you both obey those rules.
  • If you grow past quarter size while riding, you drop off automatically.
  • It follows by the shortest route, through glued doorways when that's shorter (a breadth-first search over quarter-size squares, redone a few times a second).
  • It has its own fixed door window, so sometimes it can't follow and you come back for it.
  • Flocks of quail appear at certain points as landmarks and charm. Not a collecting game.

Look and feel

The whole game is set in one region: the Chihuahuan Desert and its edges, the scaled quail's home (northern Mexico, far west Texas, southern New Mexico, into the high grasslands to the north). It's a high desert of flat basins and sudden mountain ranges, pale limestone and gypsum (White Sands), cold winters and summer monsoon storms.

  • Palette: pale stone, tan grass, blue-grey shrubs (and the quail's blue-grey), adobe, big skies with storms rolling in. Warm and hand-made, but airy and a little strange.
  • Interiors: mostly adobe: thick walls, deep doorways and windows, rounded corners. Walls hide the wraparound, and long views are placed on purpose. Distance haze where needed.
  • Spirals everywhere, from the region: agave and yucca rosettes, barrel cactus ribs that twist one way or the other, fiddleheads of desert lip and cloak ferns, the resurrection plant curling and uncurling, dust devils, the hooked seed pods of devil's claw. A log spiral survives scaling and a spiral is chiral, so it's the game's emblem.
  • Rotating things: ranch windmills (windpumps), clocks, dust devils. Their direction of turn shows mirroring at a glance, and they can drive timed obstacles.

The field journal

The name works both ways: a genus in biology, and the genus of a surface. The journal holds both.

The world stays wordless; the journal is the one place with names, styled as a naturalist's sketchbook in the game's speckled palette.

Specimens

  • Each level has a new plant or animal. You log it with a sketch and its scientific name.
  • All species come from the Chihuahuan Desert region, matched to each level's mechanic where possible. Candidates:
    • Spirals: resurrection plant (Selaginella lepidophylla), which curls into a ball in drought and unrolls when it rains; agave and yucca rosettes; lip and cloak ferns; devil's claw.
    • Handedness: barrel cactus, whose ribs twist one way or the other; desert land snails.
    • Wraparound / rings: creosote bush, which grows outward into slow rings.
    • Others: horned lizard, roadrunner, kangaroo rat, tarantula, ocotillo, prickly pear.
  • The scaled quail (Callipepla squamata) gets its own entry when you meet it.

Surfaces

Beating a level logs its topology, revealed only after you've walked it:

  • The gluing diagram: the level's fundamental domain with arrows on the edges showing which sides join and whether they flip, in the same arrow symbols used in the world.
  • The surface's name, whether it's orientable, its genus or Euler characteristic, and for scaling levels the scale factor of each loop.

Level diagrams

Each logged level shows its diagram flat, like a UV map: the level's floor plan laid out as its fundamental domain, with arrows on the edges showing how they join. This is the main journal view, and it works the same for every surface.

  • Scaling seams are coloured, with a small ×2 mark on one side, to show where the size doesn't match. A similarity surface has no consistent size at every point (going around the loop multiplies it), so the seam is the one honest place to mark the jump. Optional: shade the surface lighter toward the big side.

Later: 3D fold-up

A bonus view, added after the flat diagrams: the floor plan as a 3D simplicial complex (triangulated, walls extruded), with a slider from flat to joined that glues edge pairs one at a time.

  • Rule: join every edge pair that can be joined in 3D; leave the rest separate, marked with arrows.
  • Cylinder, torus, Möbius band: fully joined (stretched where needed).
  • Scaling seams: joined too, stretched to meet, keeping their colour and ×2 mark.
  • Klein bottle: one pair joined into a tube, the other left with flipped arrows, rather than drawing a self-intersection that isn't really there.
  • Projective plane: one pair joined into a Möbius band, the rest left with arrows.
  • Implementation: store a flat and a joined position per vertex and interpolate (e.g. Three.js). Each surface type needs its own designed embedding.

Level markers: each level is marked with a knot from the knot table (31, 41, 51, 52…), giving a wordless, ordered numbering. Knots aren't used as in-world decoration. (Nice links: knots have handedness, and a knot has a genus.)

The impasse

A timed obstacle, driven by something like a ranch windmill. Big, you're fast enough but don't fit; small, you fit but you're too slow. You pick up an item there, but being small gets you knocked back through a portal into an earlier part of the same level. You arrive smaller than you were the first time through, small enough to fit through an opening you walked past before. The item opens the way to the quail. Ride the quail back, and the impasse is passable.

The finale

  1. Tiny rider. You shrink to an eighth, smaller than anywhere else in the game, so the quail towers over you.
  2. The clasp. You fall off (the quail stumbles or bucks you) and land at its foot, at eye level with the leg band's clasp. Only now are you small enough to work it. You unlock the band, using the same size-window logic as everything else.
  3. Losing the ruler. With the band off, the quail scales like everything else. Your one fixed reference is gone.
  4. Growing together. Get back on the quail and take the growing portals together (or grow first and bring the quail through the same portals to catch up).
  5. Breakout. You both grow past what the level was built for. A doorway meant to stop you fails, and you crash through everything and out of the level. Short, easy to succeed at, more celebration than challenge. The only time a level isn't self-contained.

Structure

  • Levels are self-contained: items and the quail's position live within one level, so state stays manageable, resets are clean, and each level can be checked by a solver (solvable, no soft-locks, no accidental shortcuts).
  • Maybe later: a hub whose portals lead to the levels, or one late moment where two levels turn out to be glued together.

Engine notes

  • A Build-style portal engine (sectors joined by portals, a transform applied on crossing) or a raycaster through the level's fundamental domain. Both handle translation, flip and scaling portals.
  • Every portal is a transformation of the floor plan only, so “down” is always down.
  • Recursion through portals needs a cap. Through scaling portals the repeats shrink and fade out naturally; on a plain torus, use walls, haze, or a cap.
  • Details to handle: the player's own body (you can see your back around a loop), sprites flipping on mirror portals, collision while straddling a portal, and the quail crossing at a different moment from you.
  • Prototype (genus/): a WebGL 2 fragment-shader ray tracer. Rays cross glued gaps by applying each gap's similarity transform; walking, pushing and the quail use the same transforms in JavaScript. Outdoor walled yards under an open sky, with plain adobe walls 4 units high (murals only on the odd framed panel) (above her eyes and the camera even at double size, so nothing is ever seen over a wall). Gaps rather than doorways: a gap's width is the largest size that fits. The level is sized for double size: a start yard, a cylinder passage, a Möbius passage, a yard with a 2:1 gap glued to itself, and a pillar yard (an L of three squares whose missing square's edges are glued by a quarter turn, so there is only 270° around the pillar). Close follow camera at a height fixed to her size (V for first person). World textures keep their pixel size relative to her: smaller her, finer detail (pebbles, then grit and straw, then grains and ants); everything in the world uses four shades; she and the quail keep one look.
