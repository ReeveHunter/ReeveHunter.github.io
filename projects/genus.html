Genus

A first-person puzzle game where each level is a strange flat surface: rooms that wrap around, seams that mirror you, and portals that grow or shrink you. A quail is the one thing that never changes size.

Idea  Last updated October 25, 2026

The idea

A Doom-style first-person game: every level is a flat floor plan with heights (stairs, ledges, sills), and gravity always holds you to the floor. What's unusual is how the level is glued together. Its edges are portals that join up into a torus, a Möbius band, or a surface where some loops make you bigger or smaller. The player never sees a map of the topology; they learn it by walking, looking, and carrying things around.

It's a puzzle game, not a shooter. The main verb is picking up, carrying and putting down items.

Three kinds of portal

PortalWhat it doesThrough the doorway
WraparoundTranslation only. The world loops (cylinder, then torus). You can see around the loop.The same room repeating.
FlipMirrors the world without resizing (Möbius / Klein seams).A mirrored room.
ScalingA 2:1 or 1:2 portal: you and what you carry shrink or grow relative to the world.A bigger or smaller room.

All portals can be open doorways: you see the room beyond already transformed, so stepping through causes no visual jump. A doorway 2 units wide on your side and 1 on the far side shows the far room twice as big, because you'll be half size there. Curtains, falling water or tapering passages are optional style choices, not requirements.

Per door, the designer can make the change obvious (the far room uses the usual furniture, so it looks giant or tiny through the door) or hidden (the far room is built at the matching scale, like a dollhouse, so it looks normal; the change shows only in carried items, familiar objects, or the quail, which never scales).

Later portals combine flip and scaling. Each loop through the level has a net scale and a net flip, depending only on the route taken. In log₂ units a 2:1 portal is +1 or −1, so sizes are whole numbers and levels can be checked by a solver.

Size

  • No HUD. You learn your size only from your surroundings: familiar crates, chairs, doors, and the quail.
  • Items scale with you while you carry them. Once put down, an item keeps its size in the world as you keep changing yours. Items are how you bring a size from one place to another.
  • Interaction depends on relative size: small crates are carried, medium crates pushed or pulled, huge crates can't be moved. The same crate changes category as you change.
  • Doors have a size window. Too small: can't reach the handle or step over the threshold. Too big: can't fit through. Near the top of the window you crawl.
  • One-way sills. A threshold with a step on one side and a ramp on the other. Placed so routes toward shrinking close behind a size floor, which means walking through the wrong door can never leave you stuck too small.
  • Speed scales with size. Small you is slow.

Handedness

  • From your own view you never flip; after a flip portal, the world looks mirrored. Something that didn't come with you looks mirrored when you return to it.
  • No text in the game. Signs are simple symbols. Chiral ones (arrows, moons, spirals, footprints) get mirrored and tell you about your path; symmetric ones (circles, stars, plus signs, up arrows) mean the same however you got there.
  • Tetromino keys and locks (L/J, S/Z): a key fits only after taking a route with the right flip parity.
  • Door hinge side as a quiet, built-in chirality cue.
  • Moon phases for some puzzles or as decoration (waxing vs. waning is a mirror pair).

The quail

  • One quail buddy, met partway through the game.
  • It goes through portals with you and flips with you, but never changes size. It's your ruler.
  • Small you rides it, and it's fast. Medium you walks with it. Big you carries it.
  • It has its own fixed door window, so sometimes it can't follow and you come back for it.
  • Flocks of quail appear at certain points as landmarks and charm. Not a collecting game.

Look and feel

  • Palette from quail plumage: speckled browns, tans and creams, scaly feather patterns. Warm and hand-made (wood, plaster, dried grass) rather than cold geometry.
  • Spirals everywhere: shells, fiddlehead ferns, wind, spiraling water features. A log spiral is the shape that survives scaling, and a spiral is chiral, so it's the game's emblem.
  • Rotating things: windmills, clocks, waterwheels. Their direction of turn shows mirroring at a glance, and they can drive timed obstacles.
  • Mostly indoors, where walls hide the wraparound and long views are placed on purpose. Distance haze where needed.

The field journal

The name works both ways: a genus in biology, and the genus of a surface. The journal holds both.

The world stays wordless; the journal is the one place with names, styled as a naturalist's sketchbook in the game's speckled palette.

Specimens

  • Each level has a new plant or animal. You log it with a sketch and its scientific name.
  • Species are matched to the level's mechanic. Possible examples: a left-coiling snail (e.g. Euhadra) for flip levels; the chambered nautilus (Nautilus pompilius), whose log-spiral shell grows without changing shape, for scaling; a fairy ring mushroom (Marasmius oreades) for wraparound; fiddlehead ferns wherever spirals come in.
  • The quail gets its own entry when you meet it (species to be chosen, e.g. Callipepla californica or Colinus virginianus).

Surfaces

Beating a level logs its topology, revealed only after you've walked it:

  • The gluing diagram: the level's fundamental domain with arrows on the edges showing which sides join and whether they flip, in the same arrow symbols used in the world.
  • The surface's name, whether it's orientable, its genus or Euler characteristic, and for scaling levels the scale factor of each loop.

Level diagrams

Each logged level shows its diagram flat, like a UV map: the level's floor plan laid out as its fundamental domain, with arrows on the edges showing how they join. This is the main journal view, and it works the same for every surface.

  • Scaling seams are coloured, with a small ×2 mark on one side, to show where the size doesn't match. A similarity surface has no consistent size at every point (going around the loop multiplies it), so the seam is the one honest place to mark the jump. Optional: shade the surface lighter toward the big side.

Later: 3D fold-up

A bonus view, added after the flat diagrams: the floor plan as a 3D simplicial complex (triangulated, walls extruded), with a slider from flat to joined that glues edge pairs one at a time.

  • Rule: join every edge pair that can be joined in 3D; leave the rest separate, marked with arrows.
  • Cylinder, torus, Möbius band: fully joined (stretched where needed).
  • Scaling seams: joined too, stretched to meet, keeping their colour and ×2 mark.
  • Klein bottle: one pair joined into a tube, the other left with flipped arrows, rather than drawing a self-intersection that isn't really there.
  • Projective plane: one pair joined into a Möbius band, the rest left with arrows.
  • Implementation: store a flat and a joined position per vertex and interpolate (e.g. Three.js). Each surface type needs its own designed embedding.

The impasse

A timed obstacle, driven by something like a windmill or waterwheel. Big, you're fast enough but don't fit; small, you fit but you're too slow. You pick up an item there, but being small gets you knocked back through a portal into an earlier part of the same level. You arrive smaller than you were the first time through, small enough to fit through an opening you walked past before. The item opens the way to the quail. Ride the quail back, and the impasse is passable.

Structure

  • Levels are self-contained: items and the quail's position live within one level, so state stays manageable, resets are clean, and each level can be checked by a solver (solvable, no soft-locks, no accidental shortcuts).
  • Maybe later: a hub whose portals lead to the levels, or one late moment where two levels turn out to be glued together.

Engine notes

  • A Build-style portal engine (sectors joined by portals, a transform applied on crossing) or a raycaster through the level's fundamental domain. Both handle translation, flip and scaling portals.
  • Every portal is a transformation of the floor plan only, so “down” is always down.
  • Recursion through portals needs a cap. Through scaling portals the repeats shrink and fade out naturally; on a plain torus, use walls, haze, or a cap.
  • Details to handle: the player's own body (you can see your back around a loop), sprites flipping on mirror portals, collision while straddling a portal, and the quail crossing at a different moment from you.
  • First step: a tiny browser raycaster with one 2:1 portal, to see whether it's fun.

Open questions

  • Story beyond meeting the quail.
  • Whether sound tracks size (pitch dropping an octave per doubling).
  • Whether items can be pushed or thrown through a portal without you, so they scale and you don't.
  • Platform.
